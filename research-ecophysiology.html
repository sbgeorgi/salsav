Ecophysiology in Agrivoltaic Systems

Research Questions

This research area focuses on the micro-level interactions between plants, soil, and the altered environment created by solar panels. We investigate fundamental questions about how partial shade and changed microclimates affect plant physiology, water use efficiency, and overall crop health and productivity. How do plants adapt their photosynthetic processes? How much water can be saved? Which crops benefit the most from these conditions?

The Plant-Panel Symbiosis

Years of field experiments have revealed a remarkable symbiosis between plants and solar panels. In hot, arid climates, the shade provided by panels creates a milder microclimate, buffering crops from extreme heat and light. This protection helps plants avoid the typical "midday depression" of photosynthesis, where they shut down to conserve water, allowing them to remain productive throughout the day.[1] The results are striking: in Arizona trials, chiltepin peppers produced three times more fruit and tomatoes doubled their yield under panels, all while using significantly less water.[2]

This relationship is mutual. As plants transpire, they release water vapor that acts as a natural evaporative cooler, lowering the temperature of the overlying solar panels. Since panels operate more efficiently when cool, this plant-driven cooling actually boosts electricity production.[3] The shade also dramatically conserves water by reducing soil evaporation, with some studies showing soil moisture remaining 15% higher under panels even with reduced irrigation.[4] This virtuous cycle creates a win-win-win for food, energy, and water systems.
